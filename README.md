# Dynamic Demand Modeling using Multimodal AI

Estimating the **price elasticity of demand for Amazon shoes (size 8)** using multimodal AI
embeddings and double machine learning. This is a replication and extension of
**Victor, Bach, et al. (2025), "Adventures in Demand Analysis Using AI."**

**Team:** Jyoti Karna, Billy Saputra, Yin Yuhui, Ladityarsa Ilyankusuma.

## 1. What this project does

We take a panel of Amazon shoe listings (price, sales rank, ratings, images, text, over time)
and ask: **when the price goes up, how much does demand fall?** The challenge is that price is
not random, so we use:

1. **Multimodal AI embeddings** (text + image + tabular) to summarize each product into a dense
   vector that captures the confounders a simple regression would miss.
2. **Double Machine Learning (DoubleML)** to estimate the causal price coefficient while
   flexibly controlling for those embeddings plus competitor ("neighbor") prices.

The outcome is a sales-**rank** proxy, so the raw price coefficient is a *rank*-elasticity. We
convert it to a true **demand elasticity** using the Pareto rank–quantity law (the **ϑ**
correction, see §4).

### Headline results

- **Whole-gender baseline elasticity ≈ −0.17** (men −0.169, women −0.172; text+image, main
  spec, ϑ-converted demand elasticity). Stable across all five control specifications.
- **Heterogeneity is the real story.** Splitting by subcategory, **fashion sneakers are the
  most price-elastic** in both genders; flats/pumps the least. Women span −0.232 to −0.095, a
  spread the single −0.17 number hides.
- **"Lazy" ≈ "proper" embeddings.** Reusing whole-gender embeddings (lazy) vs training them
  within each subcategory (proper) gives estimates whose 90% CIs overlap everywhere. At these
  sample sizes the cheap shortcut is a defensible approximation.
- **Sample size governs trust.** Women subcats (430–626 products) and whole-gender models are
  reliable; men subcats (130–180) are underpowered. Treat N < ~400 as illustrative only.

Full numbers, figures, and caveats are in [`summary/README.md`](summary/README.md).

## 3. The three experiment designs

| Design | Folders | Embeddings | Sample size | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Whole-gender** | `*-whole` | Trained on the entire gender, fully fine-tuned | men 1,029 / women 2,573 train | high (one big GPU run) |
| **Lazy subcat** | `*-lazy-embedding` | **Reuse** the whole-gender embeddings, filtered to the subcat | 130–626 per subcat | free (no new training) |
| **Proper subcat** | `*-proper-embedding` | **Re-train within each subcat** (LoRA-fine-tuned) | 130–626 per subcat | high (per-subcat GPU run) |

The **lazy vs proper** comparison is the methodological core of the project: *does the expensive
within-subcategory embedding training actually change the elasticity estimate, versus the free
shortcut of reusing the whole-gender embeddings?* (Answer: not at these sample sizes — their
90% CIs overlap in every subcategory.)

In **all three designs**, the per-subcategory analysis recomputes PCA, KMeans clusters,
nearest-neighbor competitor prices, and the DoubleML elasticities from scratch; only the
*embeddings* differ between lazy and proper.

> **Why LoRA for "proper"?** Subcategories have only 130–630 training products. Fully
> fine-tuning a ~200M-parameter multimodal encoder on that few products would overfit, while
> freezing it would defeat the purpose of the experiment. LoRA (rank-8 adapters on the
> attention q/v projections, ~5% of params trainable) is the deliberate middle ground. Note
> this means lazy (fully fine-tuned) vs proper (LoRA) is not a perfectly method-identical A/B.

## 4. Methodology in brief

- **Outcome / treatment.** Δ negative-log sales-rank (a demand proxy) regressed on Δ buy-box
  price, in **first differences** (removes the lag-suppression problem the team found earlier).
- **Embeddings.** Multimodal: RoBERTa (text) + BEiT (image) + SAINT (tabular), fused by
  cross-attention into a 256-dim vector (Victor et al. Appendix G). Produced in two variants
  everywhere: **text-only** and **text+image**.
- **Estimator.** DoubleML **Partially Linear Regression**: `Y = θ·D + g(X) + ε`, two LightGBM
  nuisance learners, 5-fold cross-fitting, Neyman-orthogonal scores, standard errors **clustered
  by product (ASIN)**. Five control sets `X`: *Embeddings*, *Controls* (tabular),
  *Emb + Controls* (**main**), *Similarities + Controls*, *PCA + Controls*.
- **ϑ rank→demand conversion.** The outcome is a rank proxy, so θ is a rank-elasticity. Under
  the Pareto rank–quantity law (He & Hollenbeck 2020), `log E[Q] ≈ C − (1/ϑ)·log(rank)`, so the
  demand elasticity is `θ/ϑ`. Using ϑ ≈ 0.6 for "Clothing, Shoes & Jewelry" scales raw
  coefficients by **×1.667**. Both the raw rank-coefficient and the converted demand elasticity
  are reported everywhere (this implements Victor et al. Remark 1).

## 5. Reproducing a result

1. **Read the report:** [`summary/README.md`](summary/README.md).
2. **Regenerate all summary tables/figures:** open
   [`summary/00_summary.ipynb`](summary/00_summary.ipynb) and *Run All*. It auto-finds the repo
   root and re-reads each experiment's evaluation output.
3. **Re-run a single experiment:** open that folder's `README.md` and follow its ordered
   notebook list. The whole-gender and lazy designs run end-to-end locally; the proper design
   requires a GPU run (see each proper folder's README and the local-only `docs/RC_INSTRUCTIONS.md`).

**Environment:** Python 3, Jupyter, plus `doubleml`, `lightgbm`, `scikit-learn`, `pandas`,
`pyarrow`, and (for embedding training only) `torch`, `transformers`, and `peft` (LoRA).
